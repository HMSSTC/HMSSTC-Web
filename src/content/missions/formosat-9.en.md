## Tracking space-weather changes through ionospheric measurements and coordinated multi-satellite observations

When satellite navigation develops positioning errors or radio communications experience interference, the cause may not be equipment on the ground, but the constantly changing ionosphere hundreds of kilometers above Earth.

The Hsia Han-Min Space Technology Center participates in Taiwan's FORMOSAT-9 national space mission. In collaboration with National Central University, the Center is developing the Space Weather Ionosphere Probe (SWIP), which combines in-situ ion and electron measurements to provide more complete observations for satellite navigation, communications, and space-weather research.

From scientific payload development and system integration for FS-9A to planned multi-satellite coordinated observations for FS-9B, the Center is building on the in-orbit verification experience gained from FORMOSAT-8 and advancing compact plasma-measurement technologies. This expands space observation from a single satellite to integrated analysis across different times and altitudes.

## FORMOSAT-9A

### From electrons to ions, mapping a more complete space plasma environment

**In-situ ion and electron measurements × Scientific payload system integration**

Charged particles in the ionosphere are continually affected by solar activity and space weather. Changes in their density, temperature, and motion can in turn affect satellite-navigation positioning and the stability of radio signals.

To obtain more comprehensive ionospheric information, the Center is participating in the development of SWIP for the first FORMOSAT-9 satellite, FS-9A. SWIP combines the Electron Temperature and Density Probe (TeNeP), developed by National Cheng Kung University, with the Compact Ionospheric Probe (CIP), developed by National Central University, to measure key electron and ion parameters simultaneously.

#### Electron Temperature and Density Probe (TeNeP)｜Measuring electron variations in the ionosphere

Developed by NCKU, TeNeP uses a compact, low-power design to directly measure electron temperature and density at the satellite's position and track changes in the ionospheric plasma environment. The technology has already produced initial in-orbit observations aboard FORMOSAT-8 and continues to support instrument integration and development for FORMOSAT-9.

#### Compact Ionospheric Probe (CIP)｜Characterizing ion motion and composition

Developed by National Central University, CIP measures ion composition, density, velocity, and temperature. Working with TeNeP, SWIP can obtain in-situ electron and ion information simultaneously, enabling further analysis of the distribution, motion, and irregular variations of ionospheric plasma.

#### From instrument verification to system integration, ensuring that every measurement returns reliably from space

The Center's team completed the development and functional testing of the TeNeP engineering-verification circuitry. Working with National Central University, it also verified SWIP's power, grounding, measurement timing, and data interfaces, progressively advancing system integration between the instruments and the backend processing unit.

In addition to verifying instrument functions, the Center completed long-duration data-transmission and radiation-tolerance tests for TeNeP. These tests confirmed system performance during continuous operation and after radiation exposure, progressively establishing the stability and reliability required for the satellite mission and providing an important basis for subsequent flight-unit development and system integration.

Through precision sensing-circuit development, data-transmission verification, and cross-system integration, the Center continues to accumulate engineering experience in satellite scientific payloads and demonstrate its capabilities from individual instrument development to complete scientific payload integration.

## FORMOSAT-9B

### Relay observations between satellites to trace changes in ionospheric disturbances

**Coordinated multi-satellite observation × GNSS signal and plasma-measurement analysis**

The ionosphere is not static. The same region of the upper atmosphere may exhibit different plasma characteristics at different times or altitudes, while plasma irregularities can cause navigation-signal scintillation, positioning errors, or unstable tracking.

Building on the technical results of FS-9A, the Center and National Central University have jointly proposed a SWIP development plan for the second FORMOSAT-9 satellite, FS-9B. The plan would combine existing GNSS radio-occultation and reflectometry (GNSS RO-R) observations with in-situ plasma measurements to characterize space-weather changes from multiple perspectives.

#### From a single satellite to coordinated observations across time and altitude

FS-9B is planned to continue the integrated CIP and TeNeP architecture. Together with the different orbital configurations of FS-9A and FORMOSAT-8A, it would progressively establish cross-satellite ionospheric observation capabilities:

- **Similar altitude, different times｜** FS-9A and FS-9B are planned to observe at an altitude of approximately 514 km during adjacent nighttime periods, comparing how plasma irregularities change before and after midnight.
- **Similar times, different altitudes｜** Measurements from FORMOSAT-8A at an altitude of approximately 561 km would support analysis of differences in ionospheric plasma structures at different altitudes.

By combining observations from multiple satellites, the research team can analyze ionospheric disturbances across both time and altitude, extending the information available from any single satellite.

#### Connecting invisible plasma variations to navigation and communication signals

FS-9B is expected to compare the electron and ion states measured by SWIP with signal characteristics obtained through GNSS occultation and reflectometry, analyzing relationships between ionospheric disturbances and signal amplitude, phase, tracking stability, and data quality.

Combining in-situ satellite measurements with GNSS remote sensing can help identify signal changes caused by actual ionospheric disturbances, providing observational evidence for ionospheric model validation, space-weather forecasting research, and assessment of navigation and satellite-communication environments.

#### Building on flight experience to advance next-generation ionospheric observation technology

The planned FS-9B scientific payload will build on the in-orbit verification experience of TeNeP aboard FORMOSAT-8 and the integrated CIP-TeNeP results from FS-9A, with necessary adaptations for the new satellite platform's mechanical, power, data, and installation requirements.

The Center will continue to apply its expertise in electron-parameter measurement, payload interface integration, and scientific data analysis, together with National Central University's ion-detection technology, to explore the transition from a single scientific instrument to multi-satellite ionospheric observation.
